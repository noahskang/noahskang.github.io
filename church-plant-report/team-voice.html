§ III. Cross-City Patterns

What the responses
reveal together.

Five patterns that cut across all three cities — not specific to any location, but surfacing repeatedly in how the team thinks about this decision.

01

A2N proximity is universally valued

No one imagined planting in isolation. Whether expressed as loneliness, loss of accountability, or support for families, every respondent weighted proximity to an existing A2N network as a real cost or real asset. Atlanta wins this category. Phoenix loses it. New York is complicated.

02

Team sustainability shapes everything

Families, summers, and cost-of-living appear across all three cities' concerns. Phoenix raises heat and isolation; New York raises economics; Atlanta raises the question of whether the Bible Belt opportunity cost is worth it. The 5-year vision is only realizable if the team can actually stay.

03

College + young professional dual-track

Every vision for every city included both tracks. No one envisioned a college-only ministry — the long-arc discipleship imagination always included graduates staying and building a post-grad life in the same city. The cities differ in how naturally that track emerges.

04

Frontier vs. supported tension

Phoenix is the frontier choice. Atlanta is the supported choice. New York is a hybrid — high need, some A2N adjacency possible, extreme cost. Respondents did not converge on which cost was worth bearing. The team holds this tension without resolution.

05

A sending ethos runs through all three

Every 5-year vision includes planting another church. This is the team's shared ecclesiological imagination — not a destination church but a sending one. City choice affects timeline and difficulty, not the destination. All three cities produce this same long-arc vision.
